A Compliance Screener for Winlink Wednesdays: How It Scores, and Why It Never Transmits

Grading Winlink Wednesdays submissions by hand — checking each station's forms against the exercise requirements, confirming the right addresses were used, noting who was late — is exactly the kind of work that eats an exercise controller's whole evening. W6CSM, serving as exercise control for the ACS Area-2 series, has spent the past several sessions building an automated screener that does that first pass instead: it reads the message store, checks each submission against the published rules, and drafts a result letter. It does not decide what happens next. That's still a person's call, every time.

What the Screener Actually Checks

The rule set is deliberately literal rather than lenient-by-default. The deadline is 23:59 Tuesday Pacific time with no grace period — a submission one minute late is late, full stop. But lateness is only ever noted, never a downgrade on its own; an otherwise-complete submission that arrives late is still deemed complete. A partial submission is accepted with the missing pieces itemized in the letter, not rejected outright. Duplicate submissions are all logged, with the best of them (fewest defects) scored and lateness judged on the earliest attempt, so correcting a mistake with a resend can't retroactively make a station look late.

One rule has no exceptions: reaching both required addresses — the exercise's main list and the required copy — is mandatory regardless of everything else being perfect, and missing either one is an automatic reject. Traffic that reaches neither required address is still scored as a rejection rather than waved off as unrelated noise, a distinction that came up and was settled during the pilot itself.

Ten Exercises, Ten Rule Sets

Every one of the series' ten exercises now has its own documented content check, not just a form-type match. A few examples: Exercise 1's capability report has to show at least one entry each for band, mode, station type, and power, checked across the whole form rather than row by row. Exercise 6 pairs a FEMA resource request with a USGS "Did You Feel It" report — and includes a negative check, since the DYFI form auto-fills a USGS reply address that participants are supposed to delete before sending; leaving it in is treated as a real compliance miss, not a technicality. Exercise 10 requires a separate completion notice, sent to a specific Winlink address, on top of its communications log.

  • Deadline23:59 Tuesday Pacific, no grace period — resolved to UTC per run to account for PDT/PST.
  • PartialAccepted with a plain-language list of what's missing, not an automatic rejection.
  • DuplicatesAll logged; the best attempt is scored, lateness judged on the first try.
  • UnknownsAnything unparseable or off a form the screener doesn't recognize goes to a human hold — never auto-rejected.

Four Possible Verdicts, Never Sent Automatically

Every submission lands in one of four buckets: Accept, Accept with a noted gap, Reject, or Hold for a human to look at directly. A rejection letter always states the field, the value that was received, and what was expected — never a bare "non-compliant." Creativity and content quality are explicitly out of scope for the automated pass; every letter notes that a human content review follows separately, and nothing about the screener's verdict speaks to it.

The boundary that matters The screener does not transmit. Every letter it drafts is staged in the Winlink Outbox, and a person reviews and releases each one individually before it reaches anyone. That's not a placeholder for a future automation step — it's the design. It's also what makes running this at all defensible beyond one exercise controller's own inbox.

Across the two exercises, roughly a dozen participating stations were represented, with more submittals than stations owing to duplicates. Every single letter is still sitting in the Outbox awaiting review — the number sent by the screener itself is, and will stay, zero. The plan from here is explicitly incremental: prove it out across the county first, then extend to section-wide use, then national, if the pilot holds up under real exercise traffic. What's Still Ahead

A handful of the content-field checks described above — the band/mode/power detail on the capability report, the quantity/description check on the resource request, the weather-advisory reference on the severe weather report — are documented but not yet wired into the screener itself; the rule set is ahead of the implementation in a few spots. Letter templates for each of the four verdicts are still being refined based on pilot feedback. And there's deliberately no roster of who's expected to submit yet, so for now the screener only ever reports on stations that actually sent something — a station that submits nothing simply isn't mentioned, rather than being flagged as missing.
